## 1. Positioning

**This is a Pakistani Urdu app. Period.**

Bolo teaches the Urdu that people actually speak in Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad, Multan, and Peshawar — and the Urdu that Pakistani families speak in Toronto, London, Dubai, and Jersey City.

We make no comparisons to Hindi. We make no comparisons to Indian-region Urdu. When a phrase has multiple acceptable forms in Pakistan (e.g. `Khuda hafiz` and `Allah hafiz`), we teach both as Pakistani choices. When something exists primarily outside Pakistan (e.g. `Adaab` as a primary greeting, or Deccani forms), we simply don't include it. We aren't excluding it as wrong — it's just outside our scope.

**Voice and tone of cultural notes:** Direct, warm, matter-of-fact. *"This is how Pakistanis say goodbye."* Not *"In Muslim-majority cultures…"* or *"Compared to Hindi…"*

**Voice talent direction:** Pakistani Karachi/Lahore neutral. Not Lucknowi, not Delhi, not classical. The voice of a kind aunt or a thoughtful uncle who's lived in Karachi or Lahore their whole life.

## 2. Structure

### 2.1 Hierarchy

```
Stage          (4 total — A1, A2, B1, B2)
  └── Chapter  (60 total — themed unit, ~25 min total)
       └── Level                (4 levels per chapter, 2–3 min each)
       └── Boss Challenge       (1 per chapter, ~5 min, integrates the chapter's levels)
```

### 2.2 Why this shape

**Why short levels with a boss at the end:**

- **Short levels** = frequent wins. A user finishing a 2-minute level on the bus feels accomplishment. Habit-forming.
- **Boss challenge at chapter end** = emotional weight. The user has built up four small skills; now they apply them all in a Pakistani-context scenario. The boss is what makes a chapter *feel earned*.
- **Linear path** = no decision fatigue. No skill trees, no branches. One path, one next thing to do.

### 2.3 A level vs a boss

| Property | Regular Level | Boss Challenge |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| Length | 2–3 min | 5–7 min |
| Phrase count | 2–3 new phrases | 0 new phrases — review only |
| Exercise count | 6–10 | 10–15 |
| Format | Standard exercise mix (Introduce → Listen-to-Meaning → Listen-Repeat → Speak) | Multi-turn chained scenario |
| Failure mode | Soft fail allowed, level marked complete on retry | Must achieve ≥75% to complete; retry required if below |
| XP reward | 10 XP | 25 XP + chapter-complete bonus 50 XP |
| Visual treatment | Teal circle on the path | Larger amber/gold circle with crown icon |

## 3. Pedagogical Framework

1. **Comprehensible input first.** Hear before producing.
2. **Listening + speaking before reading + writing.**
3. **Functional, not grammatical.** Lessons organized by what the user can *do*.
4. **Spaced retrieval.** ~30% of each level reviews prior content in new contexts.
5. **Intelligibility-based assessment.** "Could a Pakistani auntie understand you?" — not phoneme-perfect.
6. **Pakistani authenticity.** Real Pakistani spoken Urdu as it exists today.
7. **No fake difficulty curves.** When something is genuinely irregular (numbers, ergative `ne`, gender agreement), we tell the user.
8. **Honor existing knowledge.** A heritage learner who knows `chacha` shouldn't be drilled on it — quick comprehension check, fast-forward.

## 4. Cross-Cutting Linguistic Threads

### 4.1 Honorifics (aap / tum / tu)

Introduced in C1 (aap), formalized in C9 (all three).

| Form | Use when |
|------|----------|
| **Aap** آپ | Strangers, elders, anyone you respect, in-laws, customers |
| **Tum** تم | Close friends, younger family, peers |
| **Tu** تو | Very close friends, siblings, children — or rude if misused |

### 4.2 Gender Agreement

Introduced gently in C1 (kaise vs kaisi), explicitly in C4 (mera/meri), drilled in C7 (colors).

### 4.3 Plural-as-Respect

Saying "hain" for one elder. Introduced in C4 (parents → `hain`).

### 4.4 Postpositions

Urdu uses postpositions (after the noun), not prepositions. Systematic introduction in C17 (directions).

### 4.5 Dative Subjects ("things happen TO me")

A defining feature of Urdu. Introduced in C13 (`mujhe bhook lagi hai` — I am hungry).

### 4.6 The Tense System

Introduced progressively across Stages 2 and 3:
- Present habitual: C22
- Past perfective with `ne`: C26–C27
- Past continuous: C28
- Present perfect: C29
- Future: C31
- Conditionals: C34

### 4.7 The Ergative `ne` Problem

Urdu transitive verbs in perfective past require `ne` on the subject. The hardest grammar in Urdu for English speakers. Introduced honestly in C26 with the framing: *"You'll get this wrong many times. That's normal."*

### **Chapter 1 — Greetings**

**Chapter goal:** Greet a Pakistani person properly, ask how they are, respond, thank them, and say goodbye.

**Cultural framing:** Greetings in Pakistan carry warmth and respect. The exchange of `Assalam alaikum` and `Wa alaikum assalam` is so woven into daily life that even shopkeepers, taxi drivers, and strangers on the street use it. Knowing how to greet someone properly is the single most important skill in Pakistani social life.

---

#### Level 1.1 — First Hello *(2 min)*

**Phrases:**

| Urdu | Roman | English |
|------|-------|---------|
| السلام علیکم | Assalam alaikum | Hello (Peace be upon you) |
| وعلیکم السلام | Wa alaikum assalam | Hello (the reply — And peace upon you) |

**Cultural note:** When someone says `Assalam alaikum` to you, replying is essentially required — not replying is considered rude. The reply is always `Wa alaikum assalam`.

**Exercise mix:**
- Introduce: Assalam alaikum
- Listen-to-Meaning: identify Assalam alaikum
- Listen-Repeat: Assalam alaikum
- Introduce: Wa alaikum assalam
- Listen-to-Meaning: identify Wa alaikum assalam
- Listen-Repeat: Wa alaikum assalam
- Speak: complete the greeting (hear Assalam alaikum, say the reply)

---

#### Level 1.2 — How Are You *(3 min)*

**Phrases:**

| Urdu | Roman | English |
|------|-------|---------|
| آپ کیسے ہیں؟ | Aap kaise hain? | How are you? (to a male) |
| آپ کیسی ہیں؟ | Aap kaisi hain? | How are you? (to a female) |
| میں ٹھیک ہوں | Main theek hoon | I am fine |

**Cultural note:** Pakistanis often follow up with `Aur sunaiye?` ("And what else?" — meaning "Tell me more, what's happening with you?"). This isn't taught in this level but it's worth knowing — the greeting is a real conversation opener, not a passing formality.

**Pedagogical note:** First taste of gender agreement. We don't explain the rule — we let the user feel the kaise/kaisi difference through exposure.

**Exercise mix:**
- Introduce: Aap kaise hain?
- Introduce: Aap kaisi hain?
- Listen-to-Meaning: pick correct gender form
- Listen-Repeat: both gendered forms
- Introduce: Main theek hoon
- Listen-Repeat: Main theek hoon
- Speak: respond "Main theek hoon" to "Aap kaise hain?"

---

#### Level 1.3 — Thank You *(2 min)*

**Phrases:**

| Urdu | Roman | English |
|------|-------|---------|
| شکریہ | Shukriya | Thank you |
| آپ کا شکریہ | Aap ka shukriya | Thank you (warmer/more formal) |

**Cultural note:** `Aap ka shukriya` is warmer than just `shukriya`. Use it when you genuinely want to express gratitude — to someone who helped you, to a host who fed you, to a stranger who gave you directions.

**Exercise mix:**
- Introduce: Shukriya
- Listen-to-Meaning: Shukriya
- Listen-Repeat: Shukriya
- Speak: Shukriya
- Introduce: Aap ka shukriya
- Listen-to-Meaning: Aap ka shukriya
- Listen-Repeat: Aap ka shukriya

---

#### Level 1.4 — Goodbye *(2 min)*

**Phrases:**

| Urdu | Roman | English |
|------|-------|---------|
| خدا حافظ | Khuda hafiz | Goodbye (lit. May God protect you) |
| اللہ حافظ | Allah hafiz | Goodbye (alternative form) |
| پھر ملیں گے | Phir milenge | See you later |

**Cultural note:** Both `Khuda hafiz` and `Allah hafiz` are widely used in Pakistan. Older generations often prefer `Khuda hafiz`; younger and more religious speakers often prefer `Allah hafiz`. Both are perfectly Pakistani — use whichever feels natural. `Phir milenge` is more casual, like "see you later" in English.

**Exercise mix:**
- Introduce: Khuda hafiz
- Introduce: Allah hafiz
- Listen-to-Meaning: distinguish both
- Listen-Repeat: Khuda hafiz
- Listen-Repeat: Allah hafiz
- Introduce: Phir milenge
- Listen-to-Meaning: Phir milenge
- Listen-Repeat: Phir milenge

---

#### Level 1.5 — BOSS: First Meeting *(5 min)*

**Scenario:** A Pakistani neighbor knocks on your door for the first time. They greet you, ask how you are, you respond, thank them for the visit, and say goodbye.

**Scripted flow:**

```
NEIGHBOR: Assalam alaikum.
YOU: → [speak the reply]

NEIGHBOR: Aap kaise hain? / Aap kaisi hain? (depending on user gender setting)
YOU: → [speak "Main theek hoon"]

NEIGHBOR: [offers something — chai, a sweet, hospitality]
YOU: → [speak "Aap ka shukriya"]

NEIGHBOR: Acha, phir milenge.
YOU: → [speak goodbye — Khuda hafiz or Allah hafiz, learner's choice]
```

**Boss mechanics:**
- 4 chained turns
- Each turn allows 2 attempts before reveal + soft-fail
- Need ≥75% score across all turns to pass
- On pass: chapter complete, Chapter 2 unlocks, big celebration

**Cultural note before boss:** Frame it as: *"You're about to have your first real Pakistani conversation. Take a breath. You know all the words. Listen carefully and respond from your heart."*

### **Chapter 2 — Introducing Yourself**

**Chapter goal:** State your name, ask someone else's name, say where you're from, express pleasure at meeting.

#### Level 2.1 — My Name Is *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Mera naam, Mera naam ___ hai
**Personalization hook:** uses the user's stored name as the template fill.

#### Level 2.2 — What's Your Name *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Aap ka naam kya hai?, Mera naam ___ hai (review)

#### Level 2.3 — Where Are You From *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Main ___ se hoon, Aap kahan se hain?
**Cultural note:** Pakistanis love asking where you're from. If you're a heritage learner, the proper answer might be "Main Pakistan se hoon" (even if you've never been) or naming a specific city. Both are warm.

#### Level 2.4 — Pleased to Meet You *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Khushi hui, Aap se mil kar khushi hui

#### Level 2.5 — BOSS: Meeting a Cousin *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** A Pakistani cousin you've never met before is introduced to you at a family wedding.
**Integration:** Chapters 1 and 2 — full greet + name exchange + pleasure expression.

### **Chapter 3 — Pronouns & "To Be"**

**Chapter goal:** All core pronouns and their matching "to be" forms. This is the foundation chapter — every subsequent chapter depends on it.

#### Level 3.1 — I, We, You *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Main hoon, Hum hain, Aap hain (with labeled meanings)

#### Level 3.2 — Tum & Tu *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Tum ho, Tu hai
**Cultural note:** A clear screen explaining when each register is used. Tu is taught with warning — among adults who aren't very close, tu is rude. With siblings, kids, very close friends, and in prayer, it's normal.

#### Level 3.3 — This, That *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Yeh hai, Yeh hain, Woh hai, Woh hain
**Pedagogical note:** Plural-as-respect previewed: yeh hai (singular near) vs yeh hain (respectful or plural).

#### Level 3.4 — Yes & No *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Haan, Nahin, Ji haan, Ji nahin
**Cultural note:** Adding `ji` before haan/nahin makes it polite. `Ji` alone, with rising intonation, means "yes?" or "please continue."

#### Level 3.5 — BOSS: Identify Everyone *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** A photo of a Pakistani family gathering. The user identifies people by pointing and saying "Yeh ___ hai" (this is my ___) or "Woh ___ hai" (that is my ___). Pronoun-and-be drilling in context.

---

### **Chapter 4 — Family (Core)**

**Chapter goal:** Immediate family vocabulary + first explicit gender-agreement drill.

#### Level 4.1 — Mother & Father *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Ammi, Abbu, Walida, Walid
**Cultural note:** Ammi/Abbu are warm and home-based. Walida/Walid are formal — used when introducing your parents respectfully.

#### Level 4.2 — Sister & Brother *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Behen, Bhai, Beti, Beta
**Cultural note:** `Beta` literally means "son" but Pakistanis use it as a term of endearment for any child, regardless of gender. An auntie calling a young girl "beta, idhar aao" (child, come here) is completely normal.

#### Level 4.3 — Mera vs Meri *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Mera bhai, Meri behen, Mera abbu, Meri ammi
**Pedagogical note:** This is the level where gender agreement becomes explicit. Show a rule reveal: *"Mera before male nouns, Meri before female nouns."*

#### Level 4.4 — Showing Respect to Parents *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Yeh meri ammi hain, Yeh mera abbu hain (note: even abbu takes hain because he's an elder)
**Cultural note:** Parents always get `hain` — this is the plural-as-respect rule. Not optional.

#### Level 4.5 — BOSS: Introducing Your Family *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** Show a family photo (user uploads or app provides a generic Pakistani family illustration). User introduces each member out loud, with correct gender agreement and respect markers.

---

### **Chapter 5 — Family (Extended)**

**Chapter goal:** The culturally distinctive Pakistani family terms.

#### Level 5.1 — Grandparents *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Dada, Dadi (paternal), Nana, Nani (maternal)
**Cultural note:** Pakistani family structure distinguishes paternal and maternal grandparents lexically. Your dad's mother is `dadi`; your mom's mother is `nani`. They have different cultural roles too — traditionally, `nani` and her side are seen as the more affectionate, indulgent grandparents.

#### Level 5.2 — Father's Side Aunts & Uncles *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Chacha (father's brother), Chachi (chacha's wife), Phuppo (father's sister), Phupha (phuppo's husband)
**Cultural note:** Pakistani families distinguish maternal and paternal aunts and uncles. There's no single word for "uncle" — you must specify which side. Heritage learners often know these intuitively from family use.

#### Level 5.3 — Mother's Side Aunts & Uncles *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Mamu (mother's brother), Mumani (mamu's wife), Khala (mother's sister), Khalu (khala's husband)

#### Level 5.4 — Family Tree Review *(3 min)*
**No new phrases.** Pure review and integration. Show a family tree, identify each relationship.

#### Level 5.5 — BOSS: The Wedding Invitation *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** You're at a Pakistani wedding. Various relatives approach. You identify each one and greet them appropriately (using L1 greetings + L5 family vocab).
**Integration:** Chapters 1, 4, 5 — heavy.

### **Chapter 6 — Numbers 1–20**

#### Level 6.1 — Counting 1–5 *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Ek, Do, Teen, Char, Paanch

#### Level 6.2 — Counting 6–10 *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Cheh, Saat, Aath, Nau, Das

#### Level 6.3 — The Irregular Teens (11–15) *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Gyarah, Barah, Terah, Chaudah, Pandrah
**Pedagogical framing:** Pakistani Urdu numbers 11–19 don't follow a clean pattern. Each one is its own word. Frame this honestly — *"This is where Urdu makes you work. Memorize, don't pattern-match."*

#### Level 6.4 — More Teens & Twenty (16–20) *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Solah, Satrah, Atharah, Unees, Bees

#### Level 6.5 — BOSS: Counting Your Family *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** "How many cousins do you have?" "I have ___." Multiple personalized counting questions integrating C4–C5 family vocab with C6 numbers.

### **Chapter 7 — Colors**

#### Level 7.1 — Invariant Colors *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Laal (red), Safed (white), Narangi (orange), Gulabi (pink)
**Pedagogical note:** These don't change for gender. Easier — taught first.

#### Level 7.2 — Inflecting Colors (Set 1) *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Neela/Neeli (blue), Hara/Hari (green)

#### Level 7.3 — Inflecting Colors (Set 2) *(3 min)*
**Phrases:** Peela/Peeli (yellow), Kaala/Kaali (black)

#### Level 7.4 — The Gender Agreement Rule *(3 min)*
**Rule reveal:** "If a color ends in -a, change to -i for feminine nouns. If it doesn't end in -a (like laal, safed), it stays the same."
**Practice:** Mixed sentences combining nouns from C8 (preview) with colors.

#### Level 7.5 — BOSS: Describing the Bazaar *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** You're walking through Anarkali bazaar in Lahore. Various colored items appear (laal dupatta, peeli kursi, neeli kitab). Describe each one out loud with correct color agreement.

### **Chapter 8 — Common Objects**

#### Level 8.1 — Around the House *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Ghar (house), Mez (table), Kursi (chair), Darwaza (door), Khirki (window)

#### Level 8.2 — Books and Tools *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Kitab (book), Qalam (pen)

#### Level 8.3 — Food Items *(2 min)*
**Phrases:** Pani (water), Chai (tea), Gaadi (car)

### **Chapter 9 — Register Practice (Aap / Tum / Tu)**

**Chapter goal:** Take the phrases the user knows and contrast them across all three registers. By the end, the user can *choose* a register based on social context.

#### Level 9.1 — Who Gets Aap *(3 min)*
**Cultural context level.** No new vocabulary. Show scenarios: meeting your father-in-law, meeting a customer, meeting your boss. All take `aap`.

#### Level 9.2 — Who Gets Tum *(3 min)*
**Scenarios:** Your cousin your age, your best friend, your younger sibling.

#### Level 9.3 — Who Gets Tu (Careful!) *(3 min)*
**Scenarios:** Your very close childhood friend (with permission), your pet, in prayer. Strong warning about misuse.

#### Level 9.4 — Transformation Drill *(3 min)*
**Practice:** Given a phrase in `aap` form, speak it in `tum` form. *"Aap kaise hain → Tum kaise ho"*

#### Level 9.5 — BOSS: Register Roleplay *(5 min)*
**Scenario:** Six rapid scenarios — meet your friend's grandmother, then your taxi driver, then your old college friend, then your cousin's young child. For each, pick AND speak the right form. Wrong register = soft fail with explanation.

### **Chapter 10 — Capstone: Meeting the In-Laws**

**Chapter goal:** Full integration of Stage 1.

#### Level 10.1 — Review: Greetings & Names *(3 min)*
Pure review across C1, C2.

#### Level 10.2 — Review: Family & Pronouns *(3 min)*
Pure review across C3, C4, C5.

#### Level 10.3 — Review: Numbers & Colors & Objects *(3 min)*
Pure review across C6, C7, C8.

#### Level 10.4 — Register Pressure Test *(3 min)*
Rapid-fire register choices from C9.

#### Level 10.5 — BOSS: Meeting Your Partner's Pakistani Family *(7 min)*
**The marquee scenario.** This is the demo lesson — polish disproportionately.

**Scripted flow (full):**
```
[Aunt opens the door at their Karachi home]
AUNT: Assalam alaikum, beta. Andar aao.
YOU: → [Wa alaikum assalam, shukriya]

[She gestures to the living room]
AUNT: Aap kaise hain? Aap ka naam kya hai?
YOU: → [Main theek hoon, mera naam ___ hai]

[Family is seated]
AUNT: Yeh meri ammi hain, aur yeh mera bhai hai.
YOU: → [Acknowledge — listening comprehension]

[Grandmother smiles]
GRANDMOTHER: Beta, aap kahan se hain?
YOU: → [Main ___ se hoon]

[Aunt offers tea]
AUNT: Chai piyenge?
YOU: → [Ji haan, aap ka shukriya]

[Leaving]
AUNT: Acha beta, phir milenge.
YOU: → [Khuda hafiz / Allah hafiz]
```

**On completion:** Major celebration. Stage 1 badge unlock. Preview of Stage 2.
